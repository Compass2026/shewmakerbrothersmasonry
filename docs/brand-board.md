## Logo

The existing mark is a full-colour illustrated badge: a bearded mason with folded arms, an American flag behind him, a stone fireplace with a mounted buck to his right, a trowel at lower left, and a John 3:16 forearm tattoo. Wordmark reads SHEWMAKER BROTHERS above, MASONRY below, in a heavy condensed slab with a printed, textured finish.

This is a strong, distinctive mark and the site should be built around it rather than modernising it. It does most of the positioning work on its own.

## Palette

Pulled from the logo artwork. These are also, not coincidentally, the colours of Ozarks limestone and the flagstone in their own project photos.

| **Role** | **Hex** | **Where it is used** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ground / primary dark | #221E1A | Page backgrounds, header, footer, dark sections |
| Sandstone / primary light | #D9C6A3 | Headings on dark, wordmark, primary light surfaces |
| Brick / accent | #B5542A | Buttons, links, calls to action, active states |
| Fieldstone / mid neutral | #8A7F6D | Body text on light, borders, dividers, muted labels |
| Mortar / light surface | #EFE9DE | Light section backgrounds, cards, form fields |
| Deep shadow | #100E0C | Overlays, image scrims, footer base |

Rules: the brick accent is for action only — never for large fills or body text. Dark sections should dominate above the fold; this brand does not read as a white, airy site. Photography sits on the dark ground so the stonework carries the colour.

Contrast: sandstone on ground and mortar on ground both clear WCAG AA at body sizes. Brick accent on ground does not clear AA at small text sizes — use it for buttons and large type, not for small links on dark.

## Typography

The logo uses a heavy condensed slab with a letterpress texture. Site type should sit in the same register: sturdy, American, slightly vintage. Not a modern geometric sans.

| **Role** | **Direction** | **Notes** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Display / H1 | Condensed slab or heavy woodtype | Should feel related to the wordmark without imitating it. Uppercase for section headers. |
| Headings / H2–H3 | Same family, lighter weight | Keeps hierarchy without introducing a third voice. |
| Body | Humanist sans with real weight | Must stay legible at length on dark backgrounds. Avoid thin weights entirely. |
| Numerals / detail | Body family, tabular | Phone number, years, measurements. |

Prohibited: Inter, Poppins, Montserrat and any default geometric sans. Those read as generic AI-built and undercut a third-generation craft brand.

Specific typeface selection is a Hold item for the design pass — the direction is set here, the faces are chosen against the real logo file.

## Positioning

### The one-line position

**Third-generation stonemasons at the Lake of the Ozarks who build the shoreline, the fireplace and the ground it all sits on — with their own crews, start to finish.**

### What makes that defensible

- Sourced: family owned, third generation, based at the lake. Stated in their own bio.
- Sourced: they self-perform masonry, stone, concrete flatwork and excavation. Confirmed at intake. Most masonry outfits sub out concrete and dirt work; most shoreline outfits do not lay stone.
- Sourced: their shoreline work is mortared stone on a footing, evidenced repeatedly in project photos — a different product from the rip rap that dominates this market.
- Unverified: number of years in business. Third generation implies decades but no figure has been confirmed. Do not publish a number until Sam gives one.
- Unverified: licensing, insurance, bonding and warranty terms. Do not publish claims about any of these until confirmed.

### The competitive argument

The shoreline market at the Lake is held by rip rap contractors, most of them attached to dock businesses. Rip rap is dumped or placed rock: functional erosion control, priced accordingly. Shewmaker builds mortared stone seawalls on a concrete footing.

The argument the site has to make, in plain terms: rip rap protects your shoreline. A stone seawall protects it and looks like it belongs to the house. That is the whole positioning wedge, and it should appear on the shoreline pages, the Lake Ozark and Horseshoe Bend city pages, and in at least one blog post.

Never disparage rip rap. Some customers need it and Shewmaker may work alongside crews who install it. The argument is about fit, not quality.

## Audience

| Lakefront homeowner, often second home | A shoreline, patio or fireplace that looks like part of the property, built once and correctly | Photography of finished work at other lake houses. Evidence the crew is local and does its own work. |
| Year-round lake resident | A repair done right and on schedule — chimney, wall, seawall | Responsiveness, plain explanation of the problem, no upsell. |
| Builder / general contractor | A mason who shows up, self-performs and does not hold up the schedule | Work with other builders, ability to handle stone, concrete and excavation on one contract. |

The lakefront homeowner is the primary. Much of that audience is affluent, frequently out of town, and buying on the strength of photographs and a phone call. That is the single biggest reason the project gallery matters more here than on a typical contractor site.

## Voice and tone

Their own captions are the reference. Actual examples:

*"Now THAT’S how you finish a log home."*

*"From bare dirt to a one-of-a-kind masterpiece."*

*"Not your average patio job…"*

*"Retaining wall and stairs tear out and rebuild!"*

*"Don’t Ignore Your Chimney — Small Problems Become Expensive Repairs"*

The pattern: short, declarative, proud of the work, a little swagger. No corporate hedging, no "we strive to exceed expectations." When they explain something to a homeowner, they lead with the consequence.

### Write like this

- Short sentences. Say the thing.
- Lead with the work, not the company. "Forty feet of stone seawall, laid on a poured footing" beats "we pride ourselves on quality."
- Name materials and methods. Homeowners buying stonework want to know it is real stone on a real footing.
- For problem pages — chimneys, failing walls — lead with what happens if it is ignored. That is how their best-performing post worked.
- Confident, not boastful. The photographs do the boasting.

### Do not write like this

- "Quality craftsmanship since…" — the exact phrase every contractor site opens with.
- "We strive to", "we pride ourselves on", "second to none", "attention to detail."
- "Passionate about masonry." Nobody says this out loud.
- Long qualifying sentences. If Sam would not say it standing on a job site, cut it.
- Anything implying they are a large operation. They are two brothers and their crews, and that is the selling point.

## Imagery

This site lives or dies on photography. The brand is visible craft, the audience buys on appearance, and the gallery is the proof. Everything else in this document is secondary to getting the images right.

#### What is needed

- Close-ups of stone texture and joint work — the detail shots that show the hand of the mason. These are usually the shots contractors do not think to take.
- Finished-project wides at lake houses, ideally with water in frame. The lake context is the differentiator.
- Before-and-after pairs, especially on seawalls, chimneys and failed retaining walls.
- The brothers and the crews working. Third generation is a people claim and needs faces behind it.
- At least one strong hero image of the stone fireplace and pizza oven — currently their best single asset.

#### Rules

- No stock photography anywhere on this site. Not one image. Stock is the fastest way to undercut a craft brand.
- No AI-generated imagery of masonry work or of people. The claim is that this is their work; generated images make that a lie.
- Warm colour treatment consistent with the palette. Do not cool-grade lakefront photos into a modern look.
- Every image on staging that is not yet a real Shewmaker photo must be a visible placeholder, listed in the run log.

Current status: the Media folder is empty. Facebook holds usable images that can be pulled as an interim set, but originals from Sam at full resolution are needed before launch. This is the critical path for the website build.

#### Hard rules

- Never quote or imply pricing. Not ranges, not "starting at", not "affordable".
- Never state years in business until Sam confirms a figure.
- Never claim licensed, bonded, insured or warrantied until confirmed in writing.
- Never invent a project, a location, a customer or a testimonial. If a page needs an example and none exists, mark a placeholder.
- Never name a subdivision, cove or development unless it appears in supplied material.
- Never claim awards, certifications or association memberships.
- Do not fabricate the family history. The third-generation line is confirmed; the details behind it are not.
- The John 3:16 element on the logo stays on the logo. Do not add faith language to site copy unless Sam asks for it.
